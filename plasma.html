Plasma-assisted combustion

Can nanosecond pulsed plasma keep a methane flame stable where it would otherwise lift off or blow out? My NASA-funded Ph.D. work studied this for the injectors of methane rocket engines.

01

Why methane, and why plasma

Methane is denser and easier to store than hydrogen, and it can be produced on Mars and Titan, which makes it attractive for reusable rocket engines. Its weak points are ignition reliability and flame stability. Repetitive nanosecond pulsed (RNP) discharges create excited species and radicals that open faster reaction pathways, a form of low-temperature plasma.

02

The burner

The experiments used a single-element inverse coaxial burner for methane/air diffusion flames, with a high-voltage electrode that drives the discharge at the nozzle, run inside a Faraday cage. Air and fuel flows, pulse voltage and frequency were all controlled, and the flames were recorded with broadband and OH* chemiluminescence imaging.

03

Four kinds of discharge

As the voltage rises, the discharge passes through corona, diffuse, filamentary and thermal-spark modes. The filamentary, non-thermal spark mode did the most for the flame.

04

What the plasma did to the flame

On the laminar flame, raising the pulse frequency from 0 to 5 kHz made the flame shorter and wider, closer to a premixed flame; the plasma could also ignite low-velocity flows and burn leaner mixtures. On faster jets it delayed both liftoff and blowout and reduced the liftoff height, with frequency the more effective lever at high voltage.

05

When it works

Higher frequency and lower jet velocity lower the voltage needed to change mode. Below a critical frequency, or above a critical jet velocity, the filamentary mode never appears and the diffuse discharge jumps straight to a thermal spark. A single parameter, pulses per residence time, describes these transitions.
